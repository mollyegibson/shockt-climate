## Summary

- **Data center hardware turned out to be a weak test of both shocks.** Servers were excluded from the 2025 tariffs, and most electrical gear reaches the East Coast via Panama, where container ships kept priority during the drought.
- **The cases still produced findings worth showing:**
  - Emissions per tonne of US server supply rose 50–120% from 2024 to late 2025 and stayed high in 2026, driven by where demand growth was met, not by the tariffs.
  - The Red Sea diversions raised sea-leg emissions on Suez lanes by 19–33%.
  - For freight bound for the Southeast and Midwest, landing on the West Coast and moving east by rail roughly halves emissions per tonne.
- **Market-wide averages hide these effects; lanes show them.** That supports building the product around a user's own lanes.
- **Proposal:** make apparel the lead product for further cases, add a tariff case where the tariffs were actually paid, and widen the persona to a sustainability consultant in any industry.

## Case 1: 2025 tariffs and AI server supply

**Findings** (DERIVED from loaded data unless noted)

| Finding | Number |
|---|---|
| Emissions per tonne of US server supply, baseline (Jan 2024–Jan 2025) to response (May–Dec 2025) | +81% as reported; +50% to +119% across weight assumptions |
| Same, baseline to Jan–Aug 2026 (after the windows) | +73% as reported; +42% to +136% |
| Share of the change from the origin mix (Shapley decomposition) | 95% (+2,652 of +2,806 kg CO2e per tonne) |
| Tonnes per month, baseline to response, by origin | Taiwan ×6.0, Thailand ×5.4, Vietnam ×2.7, Mexico ×2.1 |
| Lane intensity, response window | Taiwan by air 12.0 t CO2e per tonne; Mexico by truck 0.20 t |
| Components flown into Mexico that would cancel Mexico's advantage | about 11,390 km per tonne; by sea, about 596,000 km (never in practice) |
| Exposed electrical gear: largest change in origin share | 2.1 percentage points |

**What it means (INFERENCE)**

- **The tariffs never changed what servers cost to import:**
  - Heading 8471 was excluded from the reciprocal tariffs, back to the day they started.
  - USMCA-qualifying Mexican servers were exempt from the Mexico duty.
  - Duty actually paid was at most 0.3% of value for Mexico and zero for Taiwan. This figure came from a one-off API query; it will be shown from loaded data after the Census reload.
- **Every origin grew.** Shares moved because Taiwan, which flies its servers, grew fastest. Supply didn't leave Mexico.
- **The shock in this case is demand growth for AI servers.** The tariffs are the competing explanation, and the data rules them out.

**Caveats**

- **Estimated weights:** about 29% of 2022–26 server air weight sits in months whose value per kilogram clusters at about $400/kg, which suggests Census estimated those weights (ingestion plan, question 18). That's why results are given as ranges.
- **Not measured:**
  - components shipped into Mexico;
  - production emissions.
- **Window labels:** the chart's windows are named after the tariff timeline, and those names don't imply cause.

## Case 2: Panama drought and Red Sea diversions

**Findings**

| Finding | Number |
|---|---|
| Shock windows found by the PortWatch rule | Panama Oct 2023–Oct 2024; Red Sea Dec 2023 onward (still open at Sep 2026) |
| Container voyages Asia → US East Coast, baseline to Red Sea window | +13.7% distance |
| Sea-leg emissions per tonne, Suez lanes (India, Vietnam → East Coast) | +19% to +33% |
| Sea-leg emissions per tonne, Panama lanes (Korea, China, Taiwan → East Coast) | 0% to +3% |
| Gear's East and Gulf Coast vessel tonnage on Panama lanes (baseline) | 83% |
| Gear's overall emissions per tonne | −2.0% (Red Sea window), −3.5% (Panama window) |
| Decomposition, Red Sea window (kg CO2e per tonne) | route length +8; origin mix +69; air share −80; coast −5; inland −6 |
| West Coast share of the gear's Asian vessel tonnage | 40% → 50% |
| Gear bound for western control states, tonnes per month | 1,959 → 6,393 |
| All goods from Asia bound for VA, GA, OH unladen on the West Coast | 12.9% → 15.3%; control states held at 92% |
| Gear bound for VA, GA, OH: West Coast + rail vs all-water to the East Coast + truck | 32–50% less per tonne |

**What it means**

- **The Red Sea shock is a conflict and security shock, not a climate one.** It's large and has lasted. It hit the lanes that sail via Suez, and the observed voyages show the reroute around the Cape.
- **The Panama drought barely touched container imports.** The Canal Authority gave full container vessels priority for slots (advisory A-35-2023). The drought's damage fell on bulk and LNG carriers, much of it US exports.
- **The gear is mostly the wrong product for this shock.** Its main origins sail via Panama, so the market average barely moved.
- **The rise in West Coast landings is mostly destination growth.** Only a small part is East-bound freight switching coasts.
- **The West Coast + rail comparison is the result most relevant to decisions,** and it holds whatever the shock.

## Options to make the MVP more meaningful

1. **An apparel and footwear case** (HS 61, 62, 64), the lead candidate:
   - **Red Sea:** air share stepped up in Q1 2024 and stayed higher. Sri Lanka went from 7–10% to 14–20%, and India from 7–10% to 11–13% in 2024.
   - **Port of entry:** the East Coast share of sea tonnage fell for Bangladesh (72% → 61%), Indonesia (40% → 23%), Cambodia (34% → 23%) and Vietnam (28% → 20%). China, which reaches the East Coast via Panama, barely moved.
   - **Tariffs:** China's share of apparel import value fell 9–10 points, to Vietnam, Cambodia and Bangladesh. For footwear it fell 15.5 points.
   - **Data quality:** apparel's air weights show no sign of being estimated.
2. **A tariff case where the tariffs were paid: toys (9503) and seating (9401).** China's share fell 13 and 12 points; Vietnam and Mexico gained. A move to Mexico swaps a Pacific crossing for a truck leg, which the pipeline already measures.
3. **Reframe the existing cases:**
   - **Case 1:** a policy shock that didn't change costs, with demand growth as the shock.
   - **Case 2:** led by the Red Sea, with Panama as context.
   - **Shock types:** split into policy, climate, and conflict/security.
4. **Lead with lanes, not markets.** Show each lane's change and the user's alternatives, since averages hide the effects.
5. **Show cost next to carbon.** The Census charge and duty fields are built and await the reload. A cost per tonne for the international leg would follow, if the team wants it in scope.
6. **Optional contrast case: solar modules (854143).** After the 2025 anti-dumping and countervailing duties, Vietnam fell from 35% to 5% of import value and Thailand from 21% to 1%. A large shift in origin with little change in carbon.

**Rejected:**
- **Coffee:** Brazil's share fell before the tariff.
- **Autos through Baltimore:** the diversion isn't visible in Census port data.
- **Copper:** a timing response, which the MVP doesn't model.

## Next things to focus on

### Market and user research

- **Persona.** The proposal now has suggestions changing the persona to a sustainability consultant advising companies in any industry that import goods. Review them.
- **Risk.** A consultant advises rather than decides, which strengthens the existing risk that "the persona doesn't make these decisions". Interviews should test it.
- **Interview questions:**
  - Which levers have you recommended in the last year, and which did clients act on?
  - Who decided: logistics, procurement or sustainability?
  - What data did the client have: shipment records, spend, or neither?
  - What form does advice take: a memo, board slides, or a figure in the Scope 3 inventory?
  - When a shock hit, what was the client's first question?
- **Levers to listen for:**
  - **Supported now:** air vs sea; sea-air through a hub; West Coast + rail; rail vs truck inland; volume between suppliers; buffer stock instead of expediting.
  - **Need new data:** carrier and fuel choice (including book-and-claim); consolidation and container fill; freighter vs belly air; procurement clauses; moving clients from spend-based to activity-based Scope 3 data.
- **Competitor scan** (from the proposal): EcoTransIT, Climatiq, Pledge and others, now also through the lens of consultants' tools.

### Analysis on the data we already have

- **The proposal's proportional diversion rule.** It assigns a gain to the categories that lost share, in proportion to their losses. For servers, Mexico → Taiwan becomes 0.28 instead of 0.31.
- **Coast-specific destination states,** from the Census State/Port file. Today a lane's destinations are its origin's, whichever coast it lands on.
- **Transit time beside emissions,** from voyage durations. The proposal promises it.
- **Duty paid by origin** for Case 1, from loaded data after the reload.
- **Shares by value beside shares by tonnage,** so findings that depend on estimated weights can be checked.
- **The Results at a glance charts, rerun** for each new product, with lane-level views first.
